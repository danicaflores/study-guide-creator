## 2. Key terms and definitions

| Term | Definition (from your materials) | In plain words |
|------|----------------------------------|----------------|
| Law of demand | As price rises, quantity demanded falls (ceteris paribus). | Higher price → people buy less, if nothing else changes. |
| Ceteris paribus | Used in the law of demand: "all else equal." | We change only one thing (the price) and hold everything else fixed. |
| Change in quantity demanded | Movement ALONG the demand curve, caused by the good's own price only. | The price of the good changed, so people buy more or less of it. Same curve, new point. |
| Change in demand | A SHIFT of the demand curve. | Something other than the good's own price changed, so the whole curve moves. |
| Demand shifters | Income, prices of related goods (substitutes, complements), tastes, expectations, number of buyers. | The things that move the demand curve. |
| Substitutes | Listed as a type of related good. *Definition is general background — not from your materials:* goods used in place of each other. | Coffee and tea. |
| Complements | Listed as a type of related good. *Definition is general background — not from your materials:* goods used together. | Coffee and coffee creamer. |
| Supply shifters | Input prices, technology, expectations, number of sellers. | The things that move the supply curve. |
| Equilibrium | Where Qd = Qs. | The price where the amount buyers want equals the amount sellers offer. |
| P\*, Q\* | Equilibrium price and equilibrium quantity. | The "answer" price and quantity where the curves cross. |
| Price elasticity of demand (Ed) | Computed with the midpoint formula; reported as an absolute value. | How strongly buyers react to a price change. |
| Elastic | \|Ed\| > 1 | Buyers react a lot. Quantity changes by a bigger % than price. |
| Inelastic | \|Ed\| < 1 | Buyers react only a little. Quantity changes by a smaller % than price. |
| Unit elastic | \|Ed\| = 1 | Quantity changes by the same % as price. |
| Total revenue (TR) | TR = P × Q | The money sellers take in: price times how many they sell. |

## 3. Main concepts

### 3.1 Change in quantity demanded vs. change in demand (Objective 1)
**What it is:** There are two different ways "how much people buy" can change.
- If the **good's own price** changes, you move **along** the demand curve. That is a **change in quantity demanded**.
- If **anything else** changes (income, related goods' prices, tastes, expectations, number of buyers), the **whole curve shifts**. That is a **change in demand**.

**Why it matters:** Your professor flagged this directly: "Half the class mixed up 'demand' vs 'quantity demanded' on the quiz. Know the difference!" Expect it on the exam.

**Example:** In class, the price of coffee rose from $4 to $6, and quantity fell from 120 to 80 cups. Only coffee's own price changed, so this is a **decrease in quantity demanded**. It is a movement along the curve, *not* a decrease in demand.

**Remember:** Own price → "quantity demanded" (move along). Anything else → "demand" (shift).

### 3.2 Demand and supply shifters (Objective 2)
**What it is:** A list of the things that shift each curve.
- **Demand shifters:** income, prices of related goods (substitutes and complements), tastes, expectations, number of buyers.
- **Supply shifters:** input prices, technology, expectations, number of sellers.

Note that "expectations" shows up on **both** lists.

**Why it matters:** On the exam, you'll see a scenario and have to decide which curve shifts and why. If the change is in the good's own price, nothing shifts (see 3.1).

**Example (example created for practice):** A bakery buys a new oven that bakes twice as fast. That is a change in **technology**, so it shifts the bakery's **supply** curve.

**Example (example created for practice):** A new college dorm opens next to a pizza shop. That is a change in the **number of buyers**, so it shifts **demand** for pizza.

**Remember:** Ask: "Is this the good's own price?" If yes, there's no shift. If no, find which list the change belongs to.

### 3.3 Market equilibrium (Objective 3)
**What it is:** Equilibrium is where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied: **Qd = Qs**. To find it from equations, set them equal, solve for P\*, and then plug P\* back in to get Q\*.

**Why it matters:** This is a standard calculation problem. See the worked example in Section 4.

**Example:** Class example: Qd = 100 − 2P, Qs = 20 + 2P → P\* = 20, Q\* = 60.

**Remember:** Set Qd = Qs, solve for P, then plug P back into **both** equations to check that they give the same Q.

### 3.4 Price elasticity of demand, midpoint method (Objective 4)
**What it is:** Elasticity measures how much quantity demanded responds to a price change, in percentage terms. The midpoint method divides each change by the **average** of the two values, not the starting value.

**Why it matters:** You need to compute it **and** interpret it (elastic, inelastic, or unit elastic).

**Example:** Class coffee example ($4 → $6, 120 → 80 cups). Worked out in Section 4: |Ed| = 1, which is unit elastic.

**Remember:** Use the midpoint averages, report the absolute value, and compare it to 1.

### 3.5 Elasticity and total revenue (Objective 5)
**What it is:** TR = P × Q. Whether a price increase raises or lowers TR depends on elasticity:
- **Elastic** demand: raising price **lowers** TR.
- **Inelastic** demand: raising price **raises** TR.

**Why it matters:** This connects the elasticity number to a real business decision: "Should we raise our price?"

**Example:** Coffee: TR before = $4 × 120 = $480. TR after = $6 × 80 = $480. TR doesn't change. That fits unit elasticity: the price rise and the quantity drop cancel out. *(The "unit elastic → TR unchanged" rule is general background — not from your materials. Your notes give the elastic and inelastic cases only.)*

**Remember:** Inelastic → price and TR move the **same** way. Elastic → price and TR move **opposite** ways.

## 4. Formulas

**Which one do I use?**
- Given two equations (Qd and Qs) and asked for the market price or quantity → **Equilibrium** (set Qd = Qs).
- Given two prices and two quantities and asked how responsive buyers are → **Midpoint elasticity**.
- Asked whether revenue goes up or down → **TR = P × Q** (and check it against elasticity).

### Equilibrium
$$ Q_d = Q_s $$

| Symbol | Meaning |
|--------|---------|
| Qd | Quantity demanded (depends on P) |
| Qs | Quantity supplied (depends on P) |
| P\* | Equilibrium price |
| Q\* | Equilibrium quantity |

**Use it when:** You get a demand equation and a supply equation and are asked for the equilibrium price or quantity.

**Worked example (class example):** Qd = 100 − 2P, Qs = 20 + 2P
1. Set them equal: 100 − 2P = 20 + 2P
2. Move the P terms to one side and the numbers to the other: 100 − 20 = 2P + 2P → 80 = 4P
3. Solve: P\* = 80 ÷ 4 = **20**
4. Plug in: Qd = 100 − 2(20) = 60, and Qs = 20 + 2(20) = 60. Both equal 60, so Q\* = **60**.
5. In plain words: at a price of $20, buyers want 60 units and sellers offer 60 units, so the market clears.

### Midpoint price elasticity of demand
$$ E_d = \frac{(Q_2 - Q_1) \,/\, \big((Q_2 + Q_1)/2\big)}{(P_2 - P_1) \,/\, \big((P_2 + P_1)/2\big)} $$

| Symbol | Meaning |
|--------|---------|
| Ed | Price elasticity of demand (report as \|Ed\|, the absolute value) |
| Q1, Q2 | Starting and ending quantity demanded |
| P1, P2 | Starting and ending price |
| (Q2 + Q1)/2 | Average (midpoint) quantity |
| (P2 + P1)/2 | Average (midpoint) price |

**Interpretation:** |Ed| > 1 elastic, |Ed| < 1 inelastic, |Ed| = 1 unit elastic.

**Use it when:** You're given a price change and the matching quantity change for one good.

**Worked example (class coffee example):** P1 = $4, P2 = $6, Q1 = 120, Q2 = 80
1. % change in Q: (80 − 120) ÷ ((80 + 120)/2) = −40 ÷ 100 = **−0.40**
2. % change in P: (6 − 4) ÷ ((6 + 4)/2) = 2 ÷ 5 = **0.40**
3. Ed = −0.40 ÷ 0.40 = **−1.0** → |Ed| = **1**
4. In plain words: demand for coffee is **unit elastic** over this range. A 40% price rise caused a 40% drop in quantity.

### Total revenue
$$ TR = P \times Q $$

| Symbol | Meaning |
|--------|---------|
| TR | Total revenue (dollars) |
| P | Price per unit |
| Q | Quantity sold |

**Use it when:** You're asked how a price change affects the seller's revenue. Compute TR before and after, and check that the result matches the elasticity rule.

**Worked example (class coffee example):**
1. Before: TR = $4 × 120 = **$480**
2. After: TR = $6 × 80 = **$480**
3. Change: $480 − $480 = $0
4. In plain words: revenue stayed the same, which matches |Ed| = 1 (unit elastic).

## 5. Common mistakes

| Mistake | Why it's wrong | Do this instead |
|---------|----------------|-----------------|
| Saying "demand fell" when the good's own price went up | An own-price change moves you **along** the curve. That's a change in **quantity demanded**. | Own price → "quantity demanded." Other factors → "demand." |
| Shifting the demand curve because the good's price changed | Own price is *not* on the shifter list. | Only shift the curve for the listed shifters. |
| Putting a supply shifter on the demand list (or the reverse) | Input prices and technology affect **sellers**. Income and tastes affect **buyers**. | Ask "Does this affect buyers or sellers?" Remember that expectations is on both lists. |
| Dividing by the starting value instead of the average | That's the simple % change, not the midpoint method your course uses. | Divide each change by the **average** of the two values. |
| Leaving Ed negative and then calling −1.22 "inelastic" because it's less than 1 | Your notes say to report the **absolute value**. | Drop the minus sign first, then compare \|Ed\| to 1. |
| Rounding the % changes too early | It can push \|Ed\| to the wrong side of 1. | Keep at least 3–4 decimals until the final step. |
| Solving for P\* and stopping there | The question usually asks for Q\* too. | Plug P\* into **both** equations. Getting the same Q checks your work. |
| Thinking a price increase always raises revenue | That's true only when demand is **inelastic**. | Check elasticity first. Elastic → TR falls. Inelastic → TR rises. |

## 6. Practice questions

*Try these before you look at the answer key.*

**Multiple choice**

1. [Easy] According to the law of demand, if the price of a good rises (ceteris paribus), then:
   a) demand increases  b) quantity demanded falls  c) supply falls  d) the demand curve shifts left

2. [Easy] The price of pizza falls, and students buy more pizza. This is:
   a) an increase in demand  b) an increase in quantity demanded  c) an increase in supply  d) a decrease in demand

   a) Consumers' incomes rise  b) Smartphones become more popular  c) A new technology lowers production costs  d) The price of smartphones falls

   a) elastic  b) inelastic  c) unit elastic  d) perfectly elastic

**Short answer / application**

6. [Medium] A student writes: "When the price of coffee rose from $4 to $6, the demand for coffee decreased." What is wrong with this sentence? Rewrite it correctly.

7. [Medium] A firm learns that demand for its product is inelastic. If it raises its price, what happens to total revenue? Explain in one sentence.

## 7. Answer key with explanations

1. **Answer:** b
   **Why:** The law of demand says that as price rises, quantity demanded falls (Section 2).
   **Common trap:** d sounds right, but an own-price change moves you *along* the curve. It doesn't shift it (Section 3.1).

2. **Answer:** b
   **Why:** Only pizza's own price changed, so this is a movement along the curve, which is a change in quantity demanded (Section 3.1).
   **Common trap:** a is the exact mix-up your professor warned about. "Demand" increases only when a shifter changes.

3. **Answer:** c
   **Why:** Technology is on the supply-shifter list (Section 3.2).
   **Common trap:** a and b are **demand** shifters (income, tastes). d is an own-price change, which shifts nothing.

4. **Answer:** c
   **Why:** Buns are a related good used with hot dogs, so they're a complement. "Prices of related goods" is a demand shifter (Section 3.2).
   **Common trap:** d (input prices) is a *supply* shifter. That would apply to the hot dog maker's costs, not to the buyer's choice.

5. **Answer:** b
   **Why:** |Ed| < 1 means inelastic (Section 4).
   **Common trap:** Students sometimes flip the rule. Remember: "elastic" means a bigger reaction, so it's the number above 1.

6. **Answer:** The price change caused a change in **quantity demanded**, not demand. Correct version: "When the price of coffee rose from $4 to $6, the **quantity demanded** of coffee decreased (from 120 to 80 cups)."
   **Why:** Coffee's own price changed, so you move along the curve (Section 3.1).
   **Common trap:** Using "demand" loosely, the way people do in everyday speech. On the exam, the wording matters.

7. **Answer:** Total revenue **rises**.
   **Why:** Your notes say: if demand is inelastic, raising price raises TR. Quantity falls by a smaller % than price rises (Section 3.5).
   **Common trap:** Assuming fewer units sold always means less revenue.

8. **Answer:** Qd = **70**
   **Steps:** Qd = 100 − 2(15) = 100 − 30 = 70.
   **Common trap:** Adding instead of subtracting (100 + 30 = 130).

9. **Answer:** P\* = **15**, Q\* = **75**
   **Steps:**
   1. Set Qd = Qs: 150 − 5P = 30 + 3P
   2. 150 − 30 = 3P + 5P → 120 = 8P
   3. P\* = 120 ÷ 8 = 15
   4. Qd = 150 − 5(15) = 150 − 75 = 75. Check: Qs = 30 + 3(15) = 30 + 45 = 75. ✓
   **Common trap:** Getting 2P instead of 8P by subtracting 3P from 5P. When you move −5P to the right side, it becomes +5P.

10. **Answer:** |Ed| ≈ **1.22**, so demand is **elastic**
    **Steps:**
    1. % change in Q: (40 − 50) ÷ ((40 + 50)/2) = −10 ÷ 45 ≈ −0.2222
    2. % change in P: (12 − 10) ÷ ((12 + 10)/2) = 2 ÷ 11 ≈ 0.1818
    3. Ed = −0.2222 ÷ 0.1818 ≈ −1.22 → |Ed| ≈ 1.22
    4. 1.22 > 1, so demand is elastic.
    **Common trap:** Using starting values (−10/50 = −0.20 and 2/10 = 0.20) gives exactly 1.0, which is "unit elastic." That's the wrong method for this course. Use the midpoints.

11. **Answer:** TR before = $10 × 50 = **$500**. TR after = $12 × 40 = **$480**. TR **falls** by $20.
    **Why:** Yes, this matches the rule. Demand is elastic (Q10), and with elastic demand, raising price lowers TR (Section 3.5).
    **Common trap:** Thinking that a higher price must mean more revenue.

12. **Answer:** |Ed| ≈ **0.26** (inelastic). TR **rises** from $200 to $270.
    **Steps:**
    1. % change in Q: (90 − 100) ÷ ((90 + 100)/2) = −10 ÷ 95 ≈ −0.1053
    2. % change in P: (3 − 2) ÷ ((3 + 2)/2) = 1 ÷ 2.5 = 0.40
    3. Ed = −0.1053 ÷ 0.40 ≈ −0.26 → |Ed| ≈ 0.26 < 1, so it's inelastic
    4. TR before = $2 × 100 = $200. TR after = $3 × 90 = $270. TR rises, which matches the inelastic rule.
    **Common trap:** Dividing % change in P by % change in Q (flipping the fraction). That gives ≈ 3.8 and the wrong conclusion.

13. **Answer:** |Ed| ≈ **0.56** (inelastic). TR rises from **$2,400** to **$2,880**. Yes, raise the price.
    **Steps:**
    1. % change in Q: (240 − 300) ÷ ((240 + 300)/2) = −60 ÷ 270 ≈ −0.2222
    2. % change in P: (12 − 8) ÷ ((12 + 8)/2) = 4 ÷ 10 = 0.40
    3. Ed = −0.2222 ÷ 0.40 ≈ −0.56 → |Ed| ≈ 0.56 < 1, so it's inelastic
    4. TR before = $8 × 300 = $2,400. TR after = $12 × 240 = $2,880 (up $480).
    5. Demand is inelastic, so raising price raises revenue. If the goal is more revenue, the theater should raise the price.
    **Common trap:** Seeing "60 fewer customers" and deciding the price increase is bad. Revenue is what matters here, and it went up.

14. **Answer:** (a) **Change in demand** (a shift). (b) P\* = **25**, Q\* = **70**. (c) Both the price and the quantity went up: P from 20 to 25, Q from 60 to 70.
    **Steps:**
    1. (a) "Number of buyers" is a demand shifter, so the curve shifts. It's not an own-price change.
    2. (b) Set 120 − 2P = 20 + 2P → 100 = 4P → P\* = 25
    3. Qd = 120 − 2(25) = 70. Check: Qs = 20 + 2(25) = 70. ✓
    4. (c) Original: P\* = 20, Q\* = 60. New: P\* = 25, Q\* = 70. Both are higher.
    **Common trap:** Using the old demand equation (100 − 2P) after the shift.

15. **Answer:** (a) Q = 80 at $10, Q = 60 at $20. (b) |Ed| ≈ **0.43**. (c) **Inelastic**. (d) TR rises from **$800** to **$1,200**, which matches.
    **Steps:**
    1. (a) Qd = 100 − 2(10) = 80. Qd = 100 − 2(20) = 60.
    2. (b) % change in Q: (60 − 80) ÷ ((60 + 80)/2) = −20 ÷ 70 ≈ −0.2857
       % change in P: (20 − 10) ÷ ((20 + 10)/2) = 10 ÷ 15 ≈ 0.6667
       Ed = −0.2857 ÷ 0.6667 ≈ −0.43 → |Ed| ≈ 0.43
    3. (c) 0.43 < 1, so it's inelastic.
    4. (d) TR = $10 × 80 = $800 → TR = $20 × 60 = $1,200. Price up, TR up, which matches inelastic demand.
    **Common trap:** Assuming one straight-line demand curve has one elasticity everywhere. This same equation gives |Ed| = 1 between $20 and $30, so the answer depends on the price range.
